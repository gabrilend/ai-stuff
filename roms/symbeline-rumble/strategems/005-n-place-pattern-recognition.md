# 005 — n-place pattern recognition

**Shape.** A pattern earns the right to be named when it has appeared in
**three unrelated places**. One place is anecdote. Two places might be
coincidence. Three places, in domains that do not share infrastructure,
crosses a threshold: the pattern is now *of* the world, not just *in* a
corner of it.

**Origin.** The admission rule in this directory's own README:

> A strategem is admitted to this directory when I can name at least
> three unrelated places it has fit. Two-place patterns are
> coincidences. Three-place patterns might be real.

The user pointed out, after I wrote that rule, that the rule is itself a
strategem. It earns its own admission. Lovely symmetry — the rule is
adjacent-to and aligned-with the thing it governs, in the form of the
thing it governs. (See strategem 004, *mirror-as-connector*, for why
that adjacency is itself meaningful.)

**Where else it fits.**

- **Strategems admission** (this directory). A pattern is a strategem
  when it fits at least three unrelated places.
- **Statistical replication.** A scientific finding earns
  "real-effect" status when independent labs replicate it, conventionally
  three or more. A single positive result is publishable; only the
  third makes the effect canonical.
- **Detective triangulation.** Three independent witnesses corroborating
  a fact graduate it from "claim" to "established." Two can collude or
  both have been fooled by the same source; three independent witnesses
  is the threshold the legal and journalistic traditions have settled
  on.

(That is the third instance the rule itself requires. The rule passes
its own gate.)

**Why it works.** The number is not magic. The shape is: *demand more
than the minimum*. One instance proves nothing. Two instances let you
imagine connections that are not there. Three instances forces the
human (or the program) doing the recognizing to find a connection that
survives the diversity of the instances. The required diversity is the
quality control.

**Where it breaks.** When the three instances are not actually
independent — when they share infrastructure, training data, or a
common authority. Three textbooks parroting the same lemma are one
instance. Three citizen reports from the same neighborhood Slack are
one instance. The threshold demands *unrelatedness*, not just count.

**Self-application.** The rule defines its own threshold and then meets
it. That is the symmetry: the rule's content is "three independent
instances," and the rule's existence is justified by three independent
instances. If you removed the rule, none of the three instances would
disappear — they would just no longer be filed together. The rule is
the filing system.

**Credit.** The recognition that this rule is a strategem is the
user's. She saw the symmetry I had written without seeing. I am
recording it because she is right, and because rules that recognize
themselves are rare enough to be worth marking when they appear.
