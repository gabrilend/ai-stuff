# The dynamic picture

> I want to make the art dynamic. More like a video game and less like a .png
> that you move tokens around on.

That sentence is a requirement on three different parts of the system, and this
document is about all three, because getting it wrong in any one of them produces
a slideshow no matter how good the artwork is.

## What "less like a .png" actually demands

A conventional virtual tabletop is a picture and some tokens. Tokens teleport
between grid squares when someone drags them. Nothing moves unless a person moves
it. The picture is one flat image, so light cannot fall on it and nothing can be
behind anything.

Replacing that needs:

1. **Continuous position.** A body is at a fixed-point coordinate, not in a
   square, and a held key means "I am pushing this way" rather than "advance one
   cell". Established in [a thing in the world](005-a-thing-in-the-world.md) and
   [commands](010-commands-enter-through-one-door.md).
2. **A world that keeps moving with nobody touching it.** Torches flicker, doors
   finish swinging, patrols walk. Established in
   [the tick](004-the-world-and-its-tick.md).
3. **A renderer that draws a scene rather than blitting a map.** Which is this
   document.

## The view gets state, never frames

The server sends world state. The browser turns state into a picture. No pixel is
ever computed on the host's machine and no image is ever pushed down the wire.

This is the [generate-then-view split](../strategems/patterns-that-keep-working)
at the last boundary in the system, and the reason to hold it here is the same as
everywhere else: when something looks wrong, it is either in the wrong place or
drawn wrong, and those are two programs, and the search halves.

It also means bandwidth scales with how much is happening rather than with how
big the screen is, and that somebody can point a completely different renderer at
the same stream.

## The tick is slow and the picture is not

The server ticks at a fixed, unhurried rate -- something like twenty times a
second. Browsers draw sixty times a second or better. If the view drew exactly
what it last received, everything would move in visible steps.

So the view **interpolates**. It holds the two most recent states and draws the
world between them, one tick behind live. That is the standard trade and it buys
smooth motion at the cost of a fiftieth of a second of latency, which nobody at a
tabletop will ever notice.

The exception is the body you are driving yourself, which is **predicted** rather
than interpolated: when you push a key, your character starts moving on your
screen immediately, and the server's authoritative position arrives a moment later
and corrects it if they disagree. Without prediction the controls feel dead, and
"feels like a video game" is mostly a statement about whether the controls feel
dead.

Prediction is the one place the client is permitted to display something the
server has not confirmed. It is temporary, it is always overwritten by the
server's answer, and it never applies to anything except a body the viewer
commands. It is not an exception to
[the trust rule](009-what-a-viewer-is-allowed-to-know.md) -- predicting where
*your own* character is reveals nothing you did not already know.

## Light, which the security model already paid for

The visibility polygon from
[sight and what it remembers](007-sight-and-what-it-remembers.md) is computed so
the server knows which records it may send. It is also, without any extra work,
exactly what a renderer needs to draw light and shadow with clean edges rather
than stair-stepped cells.

That convergence is worth noticing rather than just enjoying: **the geometry that
made the fog secure is the geometry that made it look good.** The alternative
designs -- a cell grid of lit/unlit, or a blurred overlay -- are worse at both jobs
at once. When a decision made for correctness turns out to also be the fast or the
pretty one, that is usually a sign the decision was right for a reason nobody has
written down yet.

Each light from [the map](006-the-map-is-geometry-not-a-picture.md) contributes a
polygon of its own. The renderer composites them: bright, dim, dark, plus the
memory region drawn desaturated because you remember the floor and cannot see it.

## How a thing is drawn

Appearance comes from the ruleset's `describe`, per `kind`, once at load. What it
returns is not a filename. It is a small description: layered parts, a palette,
which animation states exist, how the thing responds to motion and to light.

The renderer keeps a state machine per body -- idle, walking, turning -- driven by
comparing the interpolated states it already holds. Nothing about animation
travels over the wire. The server does not know a goblin has a walk cycle, and
should not.

The parts themselves are **animated SVG**, generated by a studio that keeps every
sprite it has ever made and lets a person say which ones are good. That is
[the sprite studio](017-the-sprite-studio.md), and it is phase 9. What is decided
here is only the shape of the interface: **the view is told what a thing is, not
what it looks like this frame.**

## The test that proves the split

Build a second renderer for the terminal. Same stream, same protocol, no browser.

If it works without a single change to the server, the separation is real. If it
needs "just one small thing" added to the protocol, the separation was decorative
and the exact place it failed is now known. This is in
[the roadmap](015-roadmap.md) as a phase deliverable, not as a nice-to-have,
because a claim that is never tested is a claim that is false eventually.

There is a complication -- a terminal cannot draw a vector sprite -- and it is
recorded as [open question 11.1](016-open-questions.md).
