## 1. Why 28 of 58 and 36 of 133


The numbers themselves are the strongest clue, and they're worth reading
arithmetically rather than as "about half".


The normalisation runs over five sizes: 1, 3, 32, 33 and 64 values, totalling
133. **36 matched — and 1 + 3 + 32 = 36 exactly.** That is very unlikely to be
coincidence. It says the two smallest cases and the 32 case matched
*completely*, and the 33 and 64 cases failed *completely*.


Failing completely is itself informative. Normalisation works in two passes: sum
the squares of everything, take the square root to get one scale factor, then
multiply every value by that scale. If the sum differs in its last bit, the
scale differs, and then *every* output differs. So a whole-case failure points
at the accumulation, not at the per-value multiply.


The matrix side is consistent with the same story: 30 of 58 comparisons failed,
and 30 is exactly twice 15 — meaning cases whose row counts sum to 15 failed
under *both* kernels. The two largest cases, 8×32 and 7×65, have 8 + 7 = 15
rows. Long rows diverge; short rows don't.


So the shape is: **short accumulations agree, long ones diverge.** That is not a
logic error in the port — a wrong loop would fail everywhere, including the
1×1 case. It's a difference in the floating-point *environment* or in how a
multiply-and-add is rounded, which only becomes visible once enough terms have
piled up.


The candidates, in the order I'd check them:


- **The rounding mode and denormal handling differ.** ARM has a control register
  governing flush-to-zero and rounding; x86 has its own. Their defaults are not
  the same, and firmware sets one of them before handing over. If small
  intermediate values are being flushed to zero on one side and not the other,
  long sums drift and short ones don't.

- **A fused multiply-add crept in.** This project already found this exact
  defect once, on the *reference* side: computing the product and the sum
  together rounds once where a machine rounds twice, and the results differ in
  the last bit. I wrote separate multiply and add instructions, so this
  shouldn't happen — but "shouldn't" is what the last one said too.

### Assistant Response 10

## The ground everything stands on


Start with what a computer does when you switch it on. The processor begins
reading bytes from a fixed location and executing them as instructions. It has
no concept of "a program" — it reads a number, does whatever that number
means, and moves to the next one. Whether a stretch of bytes is code or data is
decided entirely by what you point the processor at.


Normally, what those first instructions eventually load is an operating system,
and everything you think of as software sits on top of it. This project removes
that. A chip is flashed with a language model and just enough code to run it,
and the machine is given one instruction: build every piece of software you can
fit here. There's nothing underneath it. No operating system, no compiler, no
libraries. When it wants a tool, it writes the tool.


That constraint is what makes the four open problems the shapes they are. Each
one is ordinary somewhere else and hard here, because the thing that would
normally handle it doesn't exist.


---


## One: the arithmetic that disagrees with itself


**Why it exists.** A language model is a very large pile of numbers — hundreds
of millions of them — plus a fixed recipe for combining them. To produce a
single word, the machine takes a list of numbers, multiplies it against a grid
of numbers, adds up each row of results, and repeats that a few dozen times.
Almost all of the machine's time is spent inside a handful of tiny loops doing
exactly that.


Those loops are written directly in the processor's own instructions. Not
because it's preferable, but because there's no compiler on the chip to
translate anything into them.


Here's the hardware fact that forces the next part: **a processor's instruction
set belongs to its family.** The numbers that mean "multiply these" on a desktop
x86 chip mean something entirely different on the ARM chip in a phone or a
Raspberry Pi, and different again on RISC-V. Code isn't slow on the wrong
processor — it's meaningless. So the arithmetic has to be written once per
family, by hand, three times.


Writing the same delicate thing three times raises an obvious question: how do
you know the second one is right? You can't check it by reading, because the
failure mode is subtle. Computers store fractions as approximations with a fixed
number of digits, and adding approximations is *order-dependent* — summing the
same numbers in a different sequence gives a slightly different answer. Slightly
wrong arithmetic doesn't crash a model. It makes it a bit worse at everything,
forever, for no visible reason.


So the project pins the answer down. Run the first version, record the exact bit
patterns it produced, and require every later version to reproduce them
**identically** — compared as whole numbers, so nothing rounds during the
comparison and "close enough" can't happen.


**The issue.** The ARM version agrees on short calculations and disagrees on
long ones. The normalisation step matched perfectly on lists of 1, 3 and 32
numbers, and failed completely on lists of 33 and 64.


Failing *completely* is the useful detail. That step works in two passes — add
up the squares of everything to get one scaling factor, then apply that factor
to every value. If the sum is off in its final digit, the factor is off, and
then every single output is off. So the disagreement is in the adding-up, not in
the per-value work.


Back to hardware: every processor carries a small control register that governs
how arithmetic behaves — which way it rounds, and what it does with numbers so
tiny they're near the edge of what can be represented. It can either keep them
or flush them to zero. x86 and ARM have different registers here, with different
defaults, and the firmware sets them before handing control over. If one machine
is discarding tiny intermediate values and the other is keeping them, three
numbers might add up identically while thirty-three drift apart. That's the
leading suspect.

## Two: the arithmetic that isn't written yet


**Why it exists.** Ten small routines cover everything one word of output
requires — the multiply-and-add, the normalisation, the curve that decides how
much of each signal passes through, the comparison of the current word against
every previous one. Three are written for ARM. Seven aren't, and none of the ten
exist for RISC-V.


Most of the remainder is repetitive work: loops built from multiply, add and
load, where the recorded answers tell you immediately whether you got it right.


**Where it stops being repetitive**, and this is a hardware difference rather
than a programming one: modern processors can perform several multiplications in
a single instruction, using wide registers that hold several numbers side by
side. x86 and ARM made the same choice here — a register holding four numbers,
operated on together. Translating between them takes effort but the shape holds.


RISC-V made a different choice in silicon. Its vector registers don't have a
fixed size. The program asks the chip at runtime how many numbers it can handle
at once and shapes its loop around the answer. That isn't a different spelling
of the same loop, it's a different loop. And the vector hardware is *optional*
— a given RISC-V chip may simply not have it, which is why the project's
baseline assumption for that family is one number at a time.

## Three: a computer of a fourth kind


**Why the current arrangement exists.** Nothing detects the processor, and
that's deliberate rather than lazy. To inspect a processor and choose which
version of the engine to run, you'd need code — and that code would itself
have to be written for a specific processor family, which is the problem you
were trying to solve.


So the firmware does the choosing. The firmware is a small program stored in a
chip on the motherboard that runs before anything else and knows what machine
it's part of. Modern firmware looks on the card for a file whose *name* says
which architecture it's for. An x86 machine looks for one name, an ARM machine
for another. Each finds only its own and never touches the others. There's no
detection code anywhere, because the selection happens in a filename.


**The issue.** A machine of a fourth kind — a 32-bit ARM board, an older
machine that boots the pre-firmware way, anything newer than the three — looks
for a filename that isn't on the card, finds nothing, and continues to its own
menu.


No error. No output. Nothing on screen but the firmware's own interface. From
the outside it is indistinguishable from a blank card, which is the worst
available symptom because it points at nothing.


And the obvious escape doesn't work. You can't have the machine work out the new
instruction set on arrival, because the model cannot think until an engine is
running, and writing an engine is thinking. This is the one place where the seed
genuinely isn't self-sufficient. The honest response is for the recipe that
builds a card to state which processor families it covers, so somebody knows

## Four: the read that never comes back


**Why it exists.** The machine has to discover what hardware it's attached to
and learn to operate it. Devices are controlled through memory addresses — but
those addresses aren't memory. Writing to a particular number sends a signal
down a wire to a disk controller or a network chip; reading from it asks that
chip a question. From the processor's point of view it looks exactly like
touching RAM. Physically it's a conversation with a separate piece of silicon.


**The issue is what happens when nobody's there.** When the processor reads an
address, it puts that address onto the bus and waits for something to put data
back. If a device is present, it responds. If nothing is at that address, then
on some buses **nothing responds and nothing ever will.** The processor waits.


This is not a crash. A crash means something detected a problem and reported it.
Here there's nothing to detect — the transaction hasn't failed, it simply
hasn't finished. There's no error, no message, no restart. The machine stops,
mid-instruction, and stays stopped.


Nothing prevents it, and the reason is structural. The firmware hands over a map
of which addresses are real memory, and the machine refuses to touch anything
outside it — which catches a mistyped address pointing into nothing. But
device registers are *deliberately* outside that map; that's what makes them
devices. So the check that guards against accidents can't guard the one
operation most likely to hang, because that operation's entire purpose is to go
where the map says not to.


What exists is a mitigation rather than a cure. Before touching anything
unknown, the machine writes down what it's about to do and what it expects —
to storage, before the attempt. If the attempt kills the machine, the machine
can't tell anyone; the note is the only thing that survives, and the next start
reads it. That doesn't stop the hang. It turns an unexplained dead computer into
one that left a last message.


A real fix would be a watchdog: a timer chip that resets the machine if the
machine stops periodically telling it everything's fine. That's circular in the
same way as the fourth architecture — you need to operate a device to protect
yourself while learning to operate devices — but less hopelessly, because
timers are usually reachable through a standard interface that doesn't need
discovering.


There's one more thing worth knowing, and it's uncomfortable. **The emulated
machines used for development don't hang.** An emulated bus returns all-ones
when nothing's there, and carries on. So this failure cannot be practised,
cannot be tested, and will be met for the first time on real hardware — where
it will look like a computer that simply stopped.

## ONE: why not just accept that processors add differently


You've caught something, but the premise is off in a way that's worth being
precise about — because the correction is what makes the failure findable.


**Floating-point multiply, add, and square root are exactly specified.** Given
two inputs and a rounding mode, there is exactly one correct answer, and every
conforming processor must produce it. This is not a courtesy, it's the standard.
So the same sequence of multiplies and adds on the same inputs gives
*bit-identical* results on x86 and ARM. Running it a million times on the same
chip also gives the same bits every time — it isn't noisy. Drift across
iterations comes from a *different order of operations*, not from hardware being
approximate.


Which means: **if two chips disagree on a sum of products, something specific is
different, and it's findable.** The candidates are a rounding-mode or
flush-to-zero setting left differently by the two firmwares, or a fused
multiply-add — an instruction that computes `a×b+c` with *one* rounding
instead of two. Both are legitimate hardware behaviours and both are fixable
once named.


The 32-versus-33 pattern is the evidence for this rather than against it. If
chips just added differently, the three-element case would disagree too. It
doesn't. Something crosses a threshold between 32 and 33 terms, and thresholds
have causes.

give you a slightly different machine — it gives you a *different machine*,
and you lose the ability to hand somebody an image and have them reproduce a
failure. That reproducibility is the only debugging tool the grown machine has.
