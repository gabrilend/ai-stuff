Six-Sided Dice Layer Cake

A memory-first machine for language model inference. Every figure on these sheets is derived from the design's own geometry and physics and regenerated from the blueprint set — none is typed by hand. Speaker notes sit below each sheet.

The proposition

Half the memory.
Eleven times the speed of reading it.

A 60 mm cube. Six processors on six faces, all looking inward. At the centre, a solid block of static memory holding every weight of the model — which never moves, because there is nowhere for it to go.

Sheet 01 Six-Sided Dice Layer Cake Concept Derived, not measured
Speaker notes

Open on the trade, not on the object. If they remember one sentence it should be that one — it tells them immediately what this is for and, just as usefully, what it isn't for.

Don't dwell here. Thirty seconds. The temptation is to describe the cube because the cube is the interesting bit; resist it, because a room that doesn't yet know why it should care will hear engineering enthusiasm.

The name is the shape, not a joke. "Layer cake" is the core: twenty-four tiers of memory laminated with twenty-four cooling plates. "Six-sided dice" is the six faces. If someone asks, that's the whole answer.

If they push on it — a cube has three pairs of opposite faces, and a token visits all six in order. Three of its five steps land on the face opposite the one just used, which is the most a cube permits: an ordering has to cross between opposite-pairs twice, and a crossing is always to a neighbour. It's a nice detail and it is not worth volunteering.

The problem

Inference is a memory problem wearing a compute problem's clothes.

Generating one token means reading every weight in the model, once. The arithmetic is trivial by comparison — a modern accelerator has enormous compute and spends most of it waiting.

  • The industry's answer has been to stack memory closer to the processor, which shortens the wire but keeps the arrangement: a chip, with memory beside it.
  • Every generation buys a little more bandwidth at a lot more cost, because the memory is still somewhere the processor has to reach.
  • A GPU is a general machine paying a general machine's price. It runs anything, so it must fetch anything — and the fetching is what the arithmetic waits on.

Speaker notes

Most technical investors already believe this, so state it and get agreement rather than arguing it. Ten to fifteen seconds. If you spend two minutes proving a point the room already holds, you've spent your credibility on the wrong thing.

The sentence that earns the next sheet is the last one: the arrangement hasn't changed, only the distance. That sets up the inversion as a genuine alternative rather than an increment.

Likely challenge — "what about sparsity, quantisation, distillation?" All real, all orthogonal. They shrink the model; this speeds up reading whatever the model turns out to be. Say that and don't get drawn.

The inversion

Don't move the memory closer. Put the processors around it.

  • Six compute faces, each owning a consecutive run of the model's layers. A token falls through the six in sequence, the way grain falls through a stack of sieves.
  • No DRAM. No memory bus. No off-chip traffic for weights at all — they are static memory at the geometric centre and the processors read them directly.
  • Coolant enters at four corners and leaves at the other four. Not a convenience: it is the only arrangement in which no supply channel joins two feed points.

Speaker notes

This is the sheet the pitch turns on. Give it a minute and let the drawing sit. The cross-section shows four faces; the other two are above and below the plane — say so before anyone counts and gets confused.

The sieve image does a lot of work. Six stages, one per face, and a token falls through all six. It also pre-answers "why six?" — because the model has layers and they come in order.

Likely challenge — "why SRAM? it's expensive per bit." Correct, and that is exactly the trade on the next sheet. Don't defend it here; promise the answer and deliver it in ten seconds' time.

If someone asks about the corners — the cube's edges are a bipartition, so feeding the four even corners is the one choice where every channel carries flow toward a load. It falls out of the shape rather than being designed. It's a good moment, but only if they ask.

Speaker notes

Don't read the table. Point at two numbers and move.

The pair worth naming is the two token rates: 1,021 on one sequence and 19,490 aggregate — about twenty apart. That ratio governs how the machine should be used. Below a batch of roughly twenty it's latency-bound and the arithmetic idles; above it, it's saturated and every extra sequence is nearly free. This is a serving machine, not a workstation.

The other worth naming is 59 K of thermal margin, because it is what makes the open coolant decision on sheet 09 survivable either way. Plant it here so the risk lands softer later.

If somebody wants the derivation of any figure, it exists and is one command. Offer it here and pay it off on the closing sheet.

The row a GPU person will go to first is capacity: seventy-two gigabytes usable against a comparator's hundred and forty-one. Do not defend it — it is on the next sheet as a loss, in a table with three other losses. Getting there before they do is worth more than any answer you could give here.

The hard part — 1,891 watts in 216 cubic centimetres
Sheet 06 Thermal Concept Derived, not measured
Speaker notes

Lead by conceding this is the scariest number in the deck. Two kilowatts in something the size of a golf ball sounds impossible, and a room already thinking it will not hear anything else until you say it first. A GPU rejects a comparable load across a card the size of a book. This does it through the frame, because there is no room for a heatsink.

The loop is what is designed and costed. Coolant enters four corners, leaves the other four, and every face sees fluid at the same inlet temperature — which is why the junction trace is flat. Fifty-nine kelvin of margin at the hottest point.

The line is an alternative, and say so plainly. Two pumps drive the column back and forth instead of round; while one end is outside losing heat the other is inside picking it up, and then they trade. It needs one channel per rail instead of two, which is the largest single reduction available to the cube's edge — and every dimension in the design hangs off that edge. The cost is the trace beside it: the temperature stops being level and becomes a heartbeat at the stroke frequency.

If asked which one wins: nobody knows yet, and the honest reason is specific. The deciding number is the ripple amplitude against the fifty-nine kelvin of margin, and behind it the fatigue cost of cycling every bond in the machine at the stroke rate over ten years. It is recorded as an open question rather than a decision, and it is an afternoon's work to settle the first half.

Two results worth having ready. Halving the flow does not halve the cooling — in laminar flow the heat-transfer coefficient does not depend on velocity at all, so a pump at half speed costs about four kelvin of the fifty-nine. And the coolant divides exactly evenly between the six faces, because sixteen of the sixty-four legal plumbing arrangements have a threefold symmetry that makes all six equivalent.

Sheet 07 Where it wins and loses Concept Derived, not measured
Speaker notes

Read the losing rows out loud and do not hurry them. Four of the ten go against us and one of the four — the software ecosystem — is the one that kills most hardware companies. A room that watches you present your own weaknesses in the same tone as your strengths will believe both.

The three unshared use cases, if they want them named. What this does and a GPU cannot: serve a model that fits, at very low latency, in a fraction of the volume and power — because the weights are already where the arithmetic is. What a GPU does and this cannot: run anything else at all. Training, a larger model, a different framework, a workload nobody anticipated. Where they are simply different: at the capacity edge, where a GPU pages and slows and this one declines.

On latency: the single-sequence number is the one to press if the room is interested in interactive work rather than batch. A conventional part fetches weights from stacked memory every token; this one does not fetch them at all. That is a structural latency advantage, not a tuning one.

The empty cells are deliberate and worth pointing at. Somebody will ask why the comparator column is half blank. The answer is that this project holds one bandwidth figure and one capacity figure for it, taken from a published specification, and filling the rest with plausible numbers would be the exact thing that destroys a deck in diligence.

Sheet 07b Time to parity Concept Assumption, not forecast
Speaker notes

Say the words "these are not forecasts" before anybody reads the numbers. This is the sheet most likely to be quoted back at you wrongly, and the framing has to arrive first. You are not predicting what the competition does. You are saying: here is the size of the lead, in years, as a function of a rate the room supplies.

The arithmetic is one line and worth showing on a whiteboard if pressed — how many times does a rate compound before it covers a factor of eleven. Nothing is hidden in it.

Quote the seven, not the thirteen. Forty per cent a year is faster than stacked memory has sustained, and it is the assumption most hostile to us. Leading with the least favourable number is what makes the other two credible; leading with thirteen invites somebody to halve it in their head and stop listening.

The capacity sentence is the honest counterweight and should not be dropped for time. Bandwidth parity arrives eventually and capacity parity never does, because the comparator is ahead there now and improving too. That is the permanent half of the trade.

If asked what price does: we do not have it, and sheet 09 says so. Cost per token depends on a die price nobody here can quote — no volume, no supplier, no year. Do not be drawn into inventing one.

The obvious question

If the arrangement is better, why doesn't it exist?

  • Static memory costs far more per bit than DRAM. The trade only pays if you want bandwidth more than capacity. That is a bet about what inference workloads look like — the central bet in the design, and not a fact it rests on.
  • 1,891 watts in 216 cubic centimetres cannot be air-cooled. It needs liquid driven through the structure itself, so the cooling is not a component bolted on afterwards. It is the frame.
  • Nobody has a line for this. Six faces bonded around a sealed liquid loop, with a nine-step bonding sequence that has to run in descending temperature order, is a packaging problem rather than a silicon one.

Those are the barriers, and they are the only defensibility identified so far. No patent position is claimed. Difficulty is a weaker moat than a patent and a real one, and it is worth knowing which of the two is on offer.

Sheet 08 Why not already Concept Derived, not measured
Speaker notes

The sharpest person in the room is already holding this question, and the pitch is much stronger for asking it before they do. From them, every answer sounds defensive; from you, the same answers sound like judgement.

The first barrier is the one to be most careful about, because it is where the design could simply be wrong. Name it as a bet, not as an analysis. If capacity turns out to matter more than bandwidth, this is the wrong machine and no amount of engineering rescues it.

The third is what a hardware investor will weigh hardest, and it is also the honest reason this is a design rather than a company: there is no packaging line for it, and finding out what one would cost is much of what the next step buys.

Do not oversell the moat. Saying plainly that difficulty is the only barrier identified costs nothing with anybody who matters. Claiming a patent position that does not exist would cost everything the moment somebody checked.

What we don't know

Stated directly, because it decides the next spend.

  • Nothing has been fabricated. Not a die, not a cold plate, not a coupling. Every figure here is derived from geometry and physics. Stronger than a slide deck; weaker than a prototype.
  • There is no price. The bill of materials gives cost as ratios — silicon is 77%, memory tiers two thirds of that — and refuses a currency figure. No volume, no supplier, no year.
  • Yield is the exposed flank. Only about 64% of memory tiers come off a wafer good. Making them smaller would save more than any assembly improvement, and nobody has asked whether a tier must be one piece.
  • There is no software. A GPU arrives with fifteen years of tooling. This assumes two pieces that do not exist: something to pack a trained model into the core's format, and a reference implementation to check the first token against. This is the risk that kills hardware companies, and it is not an engineering problem.
  • Two decisions remain open — water against a dielectric coolant, and whether the model it's sized around is the right anchor. Seventeen further questions are carried in the open.
Sheet 09 Risk Concept Derived, not measured
Speaker notes

Do not soften any of this, and do not let it be dragged out of you. Volunteering the weaknesses is what makes the 573 credible. A room that has to extract the risks stops believing the strengths.

On coolant: water cools about ten times better than anything non-conducting, and there are 166 joints between water and silicon at three-quarters of a volt. A leak with water is a dead cube; with a fluorocarbon it's a mess. The substitution costs about eleven kelvin of the fifty-nine available — so the design survives either. That makes it a reliability judgement for whoever owns the failure consequences, not a thermal cliff. Say that explicitly; it converts a scary-sounding open question into a business decision.

On yield: don't minimise it. It is the flank, and the design says so in its own bill of materials. The honest position is that the mitigation is known and untried.

On software, expect the hardest question of the meeting and have the honest answer ready: this is a machine that runs one shape of workload, so it needs far less tooling than a general accelerator — but "far less" is not "none", and nobody has written the two pieces it does need. If the room's thesis is that ecosystems decide hardware outcomes, this is where they will say no, and they may be right.

The ask

An engineering review.
Not a fabrication run.

The most valuable next expenditure is somebody with process and packaging experience reading the fabrication and assembly sections and saying which of the 573 requirements they don't believe.

That is a cheap conversation because this design is written in a notation a program reads. Every dimension is either a number a person chose — there are eleven — or an expression over numbers that were. Each of the 573 requirements carries the author's own sentence saying why it must hold, and all of them are evaluated in about a second. Change an assumption and watch what breaks.

Sheet 10 Next step Concept Derived, not measured
Speaker notes

Ask for less than they expect. A room braced for a nine-figure fab request and offered a specialist's reading time takes the proposition more seriously, not less — it signals you know which risk is next rather than which risk is largest.

The second paragraph is the differentiator and it belongs here rather than on a sheet of its own, because it is only interesting as the answer to "why is that review cheap?" If you have a laptop, run it. A hardware concept that answers questions about itself in front of the room is not something most investors have watched happen, and it takes a second.

Worth knowing: the notation has a category meaning a number the design wants and cannot yet produce, and the checker refuses to call the set finished while one exists. There are currently none. The design is structurally unable to overstate its own completeness.

Likely challenge — "internal consistency isn't the same as being right." Entirely correct; agree fast. What it buys is that the remaining risk sits in assumptions, which are enumerated on sheet 09, rather than scattered through arithmetic nobody has re-done. Examples if pressed: a corner block that could not contain the chambers drawn inside it; a bonding step hotter than the joint beneath it; twenty-seven hand-written unit conversions, one of them silent because two errors cancelled.

Close on the disconfirming question. Name what would kill it — a yield number that makes the memory tiers uneconomic, or a packaging house that says the bonding sequence cannot be run — and say you would rather find that out for the price of a consultancy than a mask set.
