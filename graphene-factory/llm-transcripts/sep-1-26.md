> *Q2 — Route:* Which route from raw carbon to a graphene sheet should the
> line use?
> - CVD on copper foil — Methane and hydrogen flow over copper foil held near
>   1000 C. Copper does not dissolve carbon, so the methane cracks on the
>   surface and carbon atoms stitch themselves into a single layer that stops
>   growing once the foil is covered — self-limiting, which is why the sheet
>   is one atom thick without you controlling the time. Then a polymer carrier
>   is laminated on top, the copper is etched away in acid, and the graphene is
>   floated onto whatever you want. This is the only route that naturally yields
>   continuous sheets you can cut to size.
> - SiC sublimation — Silicon carbide wafers are heated to 1300-1600 C in
>   argon or vacuum. Silicon has the higher vapour pressure, so it boils out of
>   the surface and leaves carbon behind, which reconstructs into graphene in
>   place. Highest crystal quality and no transfer step at all, but the graphene
>   is stuck to an expensive wafer and you only ever get wafer-sized pieces.
> - Graphene oxide film — Graphite is attacked by strong acid and oxidizer
>   until oxygen groups pry the layers apart; the result disperses in water like
>   ink. You cast that ink into a film of any size, then strip the oxygen back
>   off with heat or hydrazine. Cheap and arbitrarily large, but the reduction
>   never fully heals the lattice, so the sheet is riddled with holes and
>   conducts poorly.
> - Liquid-phase exfoliation — Graphite powder in a solvent is torn apart by
>   shear or ultrasound until individual layers float free. Highest tonnage and
>   lowest cost per gram, but the product is a slurry of flakes a few microns
>   across, not sheets — you would be designing a powder plant, not a sheet
>   plant.

> On the mechanism, one correction and two ways to get what you're after.
>
> **Acid plus temperature alone can't cycle.** When copper dissolves in an
> etchant it goes `Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻`, and those two electrons have to land
> somewhere — in ammonium persulfate they're absorbed by the persulfate ion,

> water," it's "copper moved into the water *and* an oxidizer was burned."

> back you must put the electrons back, and temperature doesn't supply
> electrons. The bath runs downhill in both directions.

>

>   when you want it gone and cathode when you want it back. That's copper

>   lab technique in this exact spirit called bubbling transfer: make the copper
>   the cathode in sodium hydroxide, and hydrogen bubbles nucleate *at the
>   copper-graphene interface* and mechanically lift the sheet off without

> vapour transport works hot; both wet mechanisms need the block cold. That's a

*Q1 — Face size:* How big is one working face of the copper block?
- ~100 mm — wafer scale — A face about the size of a silicon wafer.
  Everything is tractable: the plating current is amps not kiloamps, the furnace
  hot zone is small enough to hold to a fraction of a degree, and a bubble front
  only has to travel 50 mm from the centre to an edge without tearing. The
  ceiling is that the largest possible sheet is 100 mm, so any bigger product
  has to be tiled, and tiling means seams.
- ~300 mm — panel scale — A face the size of a large wafer or a small
  display panel. Roughly ten times the area of the wafer case, so ten times the
  current, ten times the gas throughput, and a hot zone that must stay uniform
  across a third of a metre — uniformity is the thing that gets hard, because
  a face with a 20 C gradient grows different graphene at its two ends. Still
  fits in a tube furnace you could buy.
- ~1 m — architectural scale — A face a metre across. A hundred times the
  wafer area. Now the copper block is a serious mass — a metre-square slab
  even 50 mm thick is around 450 kg — which makes the fixture, the thermal
  ramp, and the single-crystal growth of the billet itself all major problems in
  their own right. But it is the only option where the product is a sheet you
  could put on a window.
- Decide it later — design scale-free — Write all three release designs in
  terms of area and linear dimension as symbols rather than numbers, so the
  machines are specified as scaling laws. You then find out which mechanism
  breaks first as the face grows, which is arguably a better comparison than
  picking a size and designing to it. Costs: nothing gets a concrete footprint,
  so Phase 7 stays blocked.
