# 010 — The Scene

The narrative half. How the numbers in [the scaffold](009-the-scaffold.md) become
words in [the tome](007-the-tome.md), and the rule that keeps the words from
becoming the authority.

The whole of it, in the author's words:

> The narrative half should be the interactions of the various axes. This will
> create a scene structure that can be narrated by an LLM, who will reference the
> character of a person, place, thing, etc, mixed with the history of these things
> (narrative text, changes in axes, etc).

## The scene is data. The words are a view of it.

This is the project's oldest rule arriving where it matters most:

> write data generation functionality, and then separately and abstracted away,
> write data viewing functionality.

**A scene is generated by arithmetic.** Who was present, who was open, which axes
were in play, what moved and by how much — all of it computed by the scaffold's
rules, all of it checkable with nothing on screen.

**The narration is a rendering of that scene, and renders nothing back.** The text
never decides anything. An axis moves because the share of one over N+1 says it
moved, not because a sentence said somebody was persuaded.

Three things follow, and they are the reason the arrangement is worth having:

**The narrator can be wrong without the city being wrong.** A bad sentence is a bad
sentence. The axes are unaffected, so nothing is corrupted and nothing needs
undoing.

**The same scene can be narrated twice and the city is identical either way.** Run
it again for a better paragraph. Run it with a different model. Run it with none at
all and read the scene record directly. The world does not notice.

**The city stays computable.** A design where the text decides outcomes has handed
authority over the world to something that cannot be replayed, tested, or reasoned
about. This one can be run headless, for a thousand days, with no narrator attached,
and the result is the same city.

That last point is what makes depending on a language model safe here. It is a
**viewer**, in exactly the sense this project has always meant.

## What an interaction is

A scene is not "two people met." It is **the set of axes in play, and what each
one is doing across the actors present.**

For one axis name, look at every actor in the gathering — the place included — and
ask who carries it, at what value, and who is open. That yields a small number of
recognisable situations:

| Interaction | When | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **shared** | several carry it at close values | common ground; the thing nobody has to explain |
| **strained** | several carry it at distant values | the unlike that makes sparks — see [the scaffold](009-the-scaffold.md) |
| **offered** | a closed actor carries it, an open one does not | it will be taken up, because the closed give and the open receive |
| **withheld** | an open actor carries it and every other carrier is absent | nobody takes it, because the one who has it can only receive |
| **asserted** | only the place carries it | the room insisting on something nobody present holds |

An axis nobody carries is not in the scene at all. It is not listed, not rendered,
and not mentioned — the same structural *nothing* that draws as bare painting in
[filters and the weave](006-filters-and-the-weave.md).

**Withheld is worth pausing on**, because nobody designed it and it is the most
human thing in the table. An open person is receptive; receptive people do not
give. So somebody can carry a thing, be surrounded, and have it go nowhere — not
because they were refused, but because being the sort of person who takes things in
is the same as being the sort who cannot hand them out. That falls out of *the
closed gives, the open receives* and nothing else.

## What a scene record holds

| Field | What it is |
| --- | --- |
| `hour` | when, on the global axis from [the day and the curve](008-the-day-and-the-curve.md) |
| `place` | which block, with its natural character, its accumulated axes, and its status |
| `actors` | everyone present, each with their character and their open-or-closed |
| `interactions` | one entry per axis in play, typed as above |
| `spark` | whether two closed actors were intent on each other, and where the arc landed |
| `changed` | exactly which axes moved, on whom, from what to what |

`changed` is the outcome, and it is **computed before the narrator is ever asked**.
The narrator is told what happened and writes it up. It is not consulted about
what should happen.

## History is what makes it a story rather than vignettes

Each actor carries an **append-only record**. Nothing in it is ever edited, because
an edited history is one that cannot be trusted to explain how a thing got to be
the way it is.

| Entry | What it holds |
| --- | --- |
| an **axis change** | which axis, from what value to what value, in which scene |
| a **narration** | the text produced for a scene this actor was in |

Each entry is marked **public** or **private** — see below.
| a **minting** | the moment an axis first came into existence, and out of what |

The narrator receives the character *and* the history, which is what lets a scene
read as continuous with what came before — the same two people meeting for the
fortieth time is a different paragraph from the first time, and the difference is
in the record rather than in a flag somebody set.

It also means **the history is the answer to "why is this place like this?"** Walk
back through a block's record and you get the sequence of gatherings that made it,
each with the arithmetic and the words side by side. Nothing has to be inferred.

## Public and private, and the two phases

Every fact an actor carries — an axis value, a history entry, a minting — is
marked **public** or **private**.

| Marking | Who may read it |
| --- | --- |
| **public** | anyone |
| **private** | only somebody who knows it |

And the narrator runs in two phases, which is the whole of how discretion works
here:

| Phase | Sees | Produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **thinking** | **everything.** All facts, all history, public and private alike | what this scene is about |
| **narration** | public facts, plus private ones the reader knows | the words |

> the narrator sees everyone's history but facts about a person can be sorted into
> "public" and "private" and knowing a private thing about a person lets that be
> included in the output. There should be a "thinking" phase and then a
> "narration" phase - the thinking should include all the facts, while narration
> should only include public and known facts.

### Why two phases rather than one filtered pass

A narrator that only ever sees permitted facts writes ignorant prose. A narrator
that sees everything and writes everything leaks.

Two phases gives the thing people actually do: **you know something, you do not
say it, and knowing it changes how you say everything else.** The thinking phase
can shape a paragraph around a private fact without stating it, which is what
discretion is, and no single-pass arrangement can produce that.

### The same rule answers the filter

A filter's reading was always `(person, place) → a number, or nothing`, and the
person half does exactly what it always did:

> basically it shows the axes, right? People carry those, places carry those, all
> things carry them

So a reading for a person is **the place's public axes, plus its private ones that
person knows.** Nothing about the signature changes and nothing new is built. The
same public-and-known rule governs what the map hatches and what the tome says, so
the two halves of the screen cannot disagree about what you are allowed to know.

### The trap, stated plainly because it is easy to fall into

If the thinking phase and the narration phase share one context, the private facts
are still sitting in front of the model while it writes, and *"narration should
only include public and known facts"* has become a **request** rather than a
guarantee.

This project's rule is that a fallback is a warning and a warning is an error —
see [the shape of the code](011-the-shape-of-the-code.md). A discretion boundary
enforced by asking nicely is exactly that kind of quiet failure: it works most of
the time, and nobody notices the times it does not.

**So the boundary should be structural.** The narration phase runs against a
context that does not contain the private facts at all — only the thinking phase's
conclusions, themselves passed through the same filter. What cannot be seen cannot
be leaked, and the guarantee holds by construction rather than by instruction.

## What bounds the number of scenes

Every actor in every block at every hour would be an unusable number of scenes and
an unpayable number of narrations.

**Working ruling: a scene exists where something changed.** A gathering that moves
no axis and strikes no spark produced nothing, needs no record, and gets no words.
That is cheap to test, because the scaffold has already computed `changed` by the
time the question is asked.

Most hours in most blocks will produce nothing. That is not a failure of the
simulation — it is what an ordinary day is, and it matches the vision's own
insistence that *life is normal*: nothing is on fire, and most of what happens is
people passing each other without effect.

## What the words are allowed to be

**They appear in the tome and nowhere else.** [The map surface](002-the-map-surface.md)
forbids text on the painting — no labels, no tooltips, nothing — so a narration
lands in the tome's scrolling text pane, at the width that pane actually has. See
[the tome](007-the-tome.md).

**They are ordinary.** The rule that governed the old event system survives its
replacement intact, because it was never about events:

> Just keep it from becoming a massive story. People live in cities, so what?

A scene is two people in a bakehouse before dawn. The variety comes from there
being enormously many of them, each generated from one real gathering, rather than
from any one being remarkable.

## The spirit, and why the word matters

When an axis is minted, the **word it gets is generated rather than chosen**, and
the choice is not cosmetic:

> it applies an axis to it that might be "ashen" or "consumed" - both of those
> reflect a different spirit, and the spirit is generated on-the-fly because we
> never know how something will be until we mix it up and see

So the narrator has one job that *is* load-bearing after all, and it is the only
one: **naming.** A name entering the vocabulary is a real change to the city, since
an axis is also a filter and will be hatched across the map from then on.

That is the single place where the words touch the world, and it is worth stating
sharply because everything above says they do not. **Naming is generation.
Describing is viewing.** The narrator does both, and the two must not be confused
in the code — see [the shape of the code](011-the-shape-of-the-code.md).

## Open questions this raises

Recorded rather than guessed, in the manner of
[open questions](013-open-questions.md), where they are also filed.

**What is a "thing"?** The author's phrasing was *person, place, thing*. This
project's vocabulary has people and places and deliberately no objects — the old
key-in-a-box was cut with the event system. If a thing is a third kind of actor,
that is a new level in [the places of the city](003-the-places-of-the-city.md) and
it needs a reason.

**When does the narrator run?** The game is Lua and LÖVE; a language model is not
in the process. Narrating at play time means a network call in the middle of a
turn. Narrating ahead of time means scenes are generated in batches and stored.
The two have very different shapes, and the fallback rule applies either way: if
the narrator is unavailable the game must **say so loudly** and show the scene
record, never invent text quietly.

**How long is a narration?** The text pane is roughly 420 pixels wide and scrolls.
A paragraph, a sentence, and a page are three different games.

**What makes a fact public or private, and how does somebody come to know a
private one?** The two-phase arrangement above settles *that* the distinction
governs both the narration and the filter, but not what puts a fact on one side of
it. Whether the marking belongs to the axis, to the actor, or to the moment it was
minted is undecided &mdash; and so is whether knowing a private fact comes from
having been present when it happened, from being told, or from something else.

**How is the discretion boundary actually enforced?** Two phases sharing one
context is not a boundary. See the trap above.
