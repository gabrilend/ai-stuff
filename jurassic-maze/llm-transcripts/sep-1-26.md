## What the pieces actually are


**The floor is not the grid.** A ball has a fractional position and the floor
under it is the four surrounding cell heights blended together. One rule keeps
that honest: a cell more than one layer above or below the ball's own surface
contributes the ball's height instead of its real one. Without the upper half,
the blend ramps into a wall and balls roll *up* it. Without the lower half, the
floor tilts away over a ledge and drags the ball down the cliff face instead of
letting it fall off cleanly.


**The slope is one subtraction per axis.** Four corner heights make the patch
exactly bilinear, so the downhill direction comes out of the arithmetic for
free. The original version sampled the field at four points around the ball —
five interpolations and twenty array reads per ball per tick — and it was also
*wrong* wherever the two samples straddled a patch boundary, because it averaged
across a seam that is a genuine discontinuity. That, plus a binary search
replacing a bit-at-a-time scan for the floor under a ball, is the eight-times
speedup.


**Walls push out, corners push away from a point.** A face reflects the velocity
component along its normal and leaves the sideways component alone, so a
shallow-angle ball slides rather than stopping. The corner post between two
walls is handled separately, because the nearest point of an inside corner isn't
on either plane — skipping it lets a ball squeeze diagonally through the join,
which looks precisely like passing through solid stone.


**Speed cap.** 9 cells per second, which is 0.15 cells in one tick, well under
the 0.34-cell radius. A body that moves further than its own width in a tick
passes a wall without ever being near it, and no collision code catches that.
It's why the timestep is fixed.

The old roller had been hiding this completely. It accelerates a ball along the

so its balls move because of the smoothing, not because of the ground. No amount
of watching them would have told you that.


So the mountain is rebuilt: it descends one layer every two cells from summit to
rim, which makes every band a shelf two cells deep and every boundary a single
step. No ball is ever on a plain. And a ball is dropped with a nudge, because
that is the only thing that ever starts one.


**What keeps it going is the interesting part.** Rolling off the edge of a step,
the nearest stone is the *edge* — not the tread and not the riser. So the push
is diagonal, and it turns part of each fall into forward motion. That's why the
resolver pushes toward the closest point rather than along the face normal, and
it's the entire mechanism by which a sphere accelerates down a staircase. I
verified it in isolation: a ball pushed onto a stepped ramp at 1 cell/s was
still travelling 14 seconds later, 18 cells along and 9 layers down, its speed
oscillating between 0.9 and 2.3 as it took each step.


## Two rules decide the rest


**A face only pushes from in front.** One dot product, and it's what makes a
rectangle behave as the surface of a solid — a ball on the high shelf beside a
cliff is on the *solid* side of that cliff, so the cliff ignores it. No special
case for edges or corners.


**The stone settles last, and the stone wins.** When the stone and a neighbour
disagree, one has to give. A body inside another body is a tight-looking pile; a
body inside a wall stands on nothing and falls forever.

## The sprite is a function, not a file


805 asked "where do sprites come from, in a project with no art?" — and the
answer that fits this project is that they're generated. A sphere lit from a
fixed direction is closed form: inside the disc, the surface normal follows from
the pixel offset alone, and the brightness follows from the normal. So a ball is
a number you can change rather than an asset somebody maintains. No image file
entered the repository.


**The seam is the part worth keeping.** `075-the-sprite-baker.lua` produces
*bytes* — width, height, four channels per pixel — and has never heard of a
texture. The viewer hands them to the engine in four lines. That means a
headless run can produce a sprite and a test can read one, so there are now
assertions about what a ball looks like:


- round, and empty in the corners

- a soft edge rather than a stepped one (a ball is **six pixels across at scale
  one**; at six pixels a vector circle is a plus sign with corners)

- brighter on the side the light comes from, and the *same* upper-left the stone
  is lit from

- symmetric about the light's own axis

- grey in all three channels, so one sprite tints to every kind and every team


Three shading terms, and the third is the one that matters: diffuse, a tight
specular, and **darkening near the silhouette**. Diffuse alone gives a disc with
a gradient on it; the rim darkening is what says *sphere*.


## Two assertions earned their keep immediately


The symmetry check first reported **eighteen levels of asymmetry on a sprite
that is perfectly symmetric**. The mirror of a pixel centre almost never lands
on another pixel centre, so rounding to the nearest compares a pixel with its
neighbour — and across a specular highlight, two neighbours differ a lot. That
was the test's own sampling error reported as a fault in the thing under test,
which is the most expensive kind of false alarm there is. It interpolates the
mirrored sample now.


The shadow was written to fade from its centre outward, and that turns out to be
nearly invisible: almost all of a disc's area is in its outer half, so it was
faint everywhere and the balls went back to looking as though they hovered. It's
dense across the middle now, soft only at the edge.


Drawing the sphere also found a hole instantly — the bouncer had no palette
entry, and the deliberate magenta kept there for an unnamed kind did exactly
what it's for. That's the first screenshot I took: 900 bright magenta discs.


## The other half turned out to be a check, not a feature


I split 805, because it was two jobs sharing only a draw call. The second half
— drawing the world from the model — was motivated by a real risk: the world
is *drawn* by a sweep over columns and *collided with* as a model of flat
rectangles. Two descriptions of the same stone, built by entirely different
reasoning, and nothing compared them. A disagreement would be a ball bouncing
off something that isn't where it's drawn, which is the hardest kind of bug
there is because both halves look correct on their own.


So I built the comparison instead of the renderer. The test sweeps the renderer
over the whole mountainside, gathers what it claims about every column top and
every face between two columns, and holds it against what the model claims about
the same edges. **They agree everywhere.**


That changes what a second renderer is *for*. It was going to be the way of
finding that disagreement; having found it another way, it's now a choice about
